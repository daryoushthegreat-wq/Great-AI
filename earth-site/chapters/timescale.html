The Geological Timescale

The calendar of Earth history, and how small our part of it is.

Geologists divide Earth history into a hierarchy of units: eons, eras, periods and epochs. The boundaries usually mark major changes in the fossil record, such as the appearance of new groups of organisms or mass extinctions. The names are agreed internationally and the dates are refined as dating methods improve.

The big divisions

Precambrian (about 4,540 to 539 million years ago). Almost nine-tenths of Earth history. Life appears at least 3.5 billion years ago, but for most of this time it is microscopic. Oxygen builds up in the atmosphere around 2.4 billion years ago.

Palaeozoic era (539 to 252 million years ago), "ancient life". Animals with shells and skeletons appear in the Cambrian. Fish, land plants, insects, amphibians and early reptiles follow. It ends with the largest mass extinction known, at the end of the Permian.

Mesozoic era (252 to 66 million years ago), "middle life". This is the age of dinosaurs, including the Triassic, Jurassic and Cretaceous periods. Pangaea breaks apart. It ends when an asteroid strikes what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula.

Cenozoic era (66 million years ago to today), "new life". Mammals and flowering plants spread. The Quaternary period, the last 2.58 million years, brings repeated ice ages and the rise of our own species. We live in the Holocene epoch, which began about 11,700 years ago at the end of the last glacial period.

Science today

Many older charts used the name "Tertiary" and dated the start of the Cambrian to about 570 million years ago. The International Commission on Stratigraphy now divides the Tertiary into the Palaeogene and Neogene periods and dates the start of the Cambrian to about 538.8 million years ago.

If the whole history of the Earth were squeezed into one calendar year, the dinosaurs would die out on about 26 December, and our own species would appear only about half an hour before midnight on 31 December.

Key terms

geological timescale
The calendar geologists use to divide the earth’s history into eras and periods.
era
A very long division of geological time, made up of several periods.
period
A division of an era, such as the Jurassic period.
Precambrian
All of the earth’s history before the Cambrian period. It is by far the longest part.
Palaeozoic
The era of "ancient life", from the Cambrian to the Permian period.
Mesozoic
The era of "middle life", from the Triassic to the Cretaceous. It was the age of the dinosaurs.
Cenozoic
The era of "new life", from 66 million years ago to today.
Jurassic
The middle period of the Mesozoic era, when dinosaurs roamed the land.
Holocene
The most recent part of the Quaternary period: the one we live in now.

Check your understanding

Think of your answer, then open each question to compare.

Roughly what share of Earth history is the Precambrian?

Almost nine-tenths.

What ended the Mesozoic era?

An asteroid impact about 66 million years ago, which wiped out the dinosaurs apart from birds.

Which epoch do we live in, and when did it begin?

The Holocene, which began about 11,700 years ago.
